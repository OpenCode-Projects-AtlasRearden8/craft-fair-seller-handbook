Honest Sales Tracking

Everything else in this book — restock math, event selection, pricing, the weather plan — runs on one fuel: a truthful record of what actually sold, when, and for how much. This chapter builds that record in seven columns and one habit. The habit is the hard part; the seven columns are easy.

Why "honest" is in the chapter title

Two ways to track sales, and only one of them teaches you anything:

  • The hopeful way: "I think we did about $300?" This number is a feeling. Feelings steer restock wrong, price wrong, and next-event decisions wrong, and they're never challenged at the till — they're challenged in January, at tax time, too late.
  • The honest way: every sale, to the item, reconciled to the money in the box and the reader app. Ten minutes of discipline buying a whole season of correct decisions.

"Honest" also means honest to yourself in the specific, awkward sense: the cash box is the business's wallet, not yours. Pay yourself at close, in one decision, per the end-of-day count — not mid-show "I'll pay myself back for lunch with a twenty." Lunch is an expense line; the twenty from the box is an informal loan against takings you haven't counted yet. One of those methods produces clean bookkeeping; the other one produces "how was I short again?"

The tally sheet (one per event)

Plain, printable, and the document of the day:

#ItemQtyPriceCashCardNotes
1Candle, medium21515
2Candle, small3515one refunded? no
3Soap bundle12525QR! signal died
4Wood ornament210515came back for seconds

The columns:

  1. # — the running row. Don't renumber; don't reorganize.
  2. Item — your product name, exactly as it appears on your restock list (see Restock math); not "the blue ones."
  3. Qty — units, not lines. A bundle of three soaps at $25 is qty 1 bundle; your restock list will count bundles and singles separately.
  4. Price — the total of that line, not per unit.
  5. Cash — the cash portion.
  6. Card — the card portion (or "QR" or "offline" if you used them; the point is that the lane is named).
  7. Notes — what you'd want to know at review time. "Ran out at 1 p.m.," "bought 12 for stamp club," "traded with neighbor booth (see below)."

Seven columns, plus the totals footer at the bottom — tally in pencil, it's faster than any app in direct sunlight with gloves on.

The totals that matter (10 p.m. version)

At close, the sheet gets a footer. Six numbers, all subtraction:

Day's CASH takings        (from the end-of-day count)  $ ________
Day's CARD takings        (from the reader app)        $ ________
GROSS SALES               (cash + card)                $ ________
REFUNDS handed back out                                − $ ________
NET SALES                 (gross − refunds)            $ ________
EVENT COSTS (booth fee + parking + tax set-aside)      − $ ________
MARGIN OF THE DAY         (net − costs)                $ ________

The margin-of-the-day is this event's real contribution: what it added to the year so far, not what it looked like at noon. The costs line belongs to the whole event — the booth fee was agreed in January; the tax set-aside is a habit, not pain. (More on taxes as a set-aside, below.)

If the day's cash doesn't equal the sheet's cash column, you count again before writing the sheet's totals down — and if it truly doesn't match, the over/short gets logged in Notes, openly. (That's the whole meaning of "honest": the number exists even when it's awkward.)

Tracking tax as a set-aside, not an opinion

Your tax answer from the permits chapter determines one of two bookkeeping habits, both easy:

  • Tax collected separately (add-on tag prices): record it at the end of the day from your till math: one line on the sheet — "sales tax collected: $X" — and it goes into an envelope the moment you're home. It is not part of "margin of the day."
  • Tax baked in (tax-included tag prices): at close, subtract the pre-tax rate from each sale in the sheet's footer only — a single arithmetic line that produces "how much of today's takings is actually the state's." Again: one footnote line, one envelope.

Either way, the same principle: the tax money is separated from your money at the earliest moment your bookkeeping sees it, so that a month later, the remittance day is dull instead of dramatic. (If your state's answer was "exempt / no sales tax here," your footnote says so, and the envelope stays empty. The habit is the habit regardless of the amount — it's a set-aside budget, not a tax opinion.)

The weekly roll-up (15 minutes)

At the end of event week — however many events your week contained, including any The Side-Hustle Reseller Handbook listings you cleared online — one page in a folder or a simple spreadsheet gets a line per event:

DateEventGrossRefundsCostsMarginRating (1-5)
May 3Farmers market, week 62200251954
May 4Spring fair, day 1540151503755

That rating column is the event scorecard score — your rating, from filling the scorecard at review time, not a sales number with feelings-adjacent spin. Roll-ups over a season are the difference between "I think holiday markets are better for me" and "holiday markets averaged a 4.4 and $60 more per hour, verified," which are such different sentences that the second one changes your bookings.

Backups, briefly

One copy is zero copies. Paper tally sheets go — same night, when you enter the roll-up line — into one of:

  • a photograph (into a folder named by date — events/2026-05-04/)
  • a typed version, into a spreadsheet
  • both (recommended; it's two minutes)

For record-keeping at this size you don't need subscription apps: paper + photo + one spreadsheet is a small business's record-keeping, and it's what professionals mean by "keeping records" in the boring, honorable sense. As for anything that needs an accountant's judgment: records kept this way are exactly the artifact a professional (or your future self) will want to see — the reseller book's records chapter and this one arrive at the same single-table philosophy by different doors.

The five rows that pay for the whole system

When a season's tally sheets stack up, these are the questions they answer — write these on the sheet's back page when you're designing it, so the data supports them at review time:

  1. What hour of the day did sales happen? Some booths live 9 a.m. to noon; some don't wake up until 2 p.m. (Tally by hour in Notes or a small hourly tally box if you want a picture of it.)
  2. Which product sold at which price raft? Small/medium/large rafts work only if you know which raft carried the day.
  3. What did people ask for that you don't make? ("Do you have it in blue?" — if three people per event ask, that's either a note to your tracker or a new product decision — this is where markets feed the online listings the reseller handbook talks about.)
  4. What did you nearly sell out of by 1 p.m.? Restock breaker (also the raw material for restock math next event).
  5. What did nobody touch? The dead stock line, which becomes a pricing or placement problem — or an honest product-sunset decision — not a guilt object.

None of this needs a scanner app, a subscription, or a business method. It needs a pencil, ten minutes at close, and the honesty to write the awkward numbers down.
