Restock Math

Selling out is a mixed blessing. It clears your table and it costs you your best hours: the customers who arrive at 2 p.m. to buy the very thing you sold at 11 a.m. are unrealized revenue — and sometimes they won't return another day. Restock math is the forty seconds of arithmetic that turns last event's tally sheet into this event's packing list.

The honest unit: sell-through per hour

From your tracking tally, compute one number per product line, in round terms:

sell-through rate = units sold ÷ hours open

Ten candles sold in an 8-hour day: about 1.25 candles per hour. If the day was honestly dead for 5 of its 8 hours (note hour-sells and you'll see it), the useful rate is units ÷ 3 active hours — about 3.3 per hour — because what you're matching is the next event's crowd, not the next event's clock. Both rates are useful; write rate-per-hour and rate-per-busy-hour side by side and let the event type decide which one to steer by (weekly markets are steady-rate events; one-day festivals are busy-hour events).

Units to bring: the bring-formula

For each line, next event:

UNITS TO BRING = rate/hour × event hours × crowd factor × 1.3
  • rate/hour — the sell-through rate above (use the low or middle one; use the busy-hour rate for festivals).
  • event hours — hours open, not hours you stand there.
  • crowd factor — how much bigger or smaller this event's traffic is than the one that taught you the rate. Same market, same season: 1.0. Organizer claims "double last year," and you believe them: 1.5 at most. Untested venue: 1.0 until proven — your first event somewhere uses a rate of 0, which is why first events are brought by shelf rules (below) rather than math.
  • × 1.3 — the buffer third: part insurance, part realistic restock. It exists because "I sold out by noon" always overestimates demand in memory, and because table gaps look worse than under-stock feels.

Worked example, deliberately round: candles sell 1.25/hour at the Spring Fair; the next booking is the same market next month, 7 hours, crowd factor 1.

BRING = 1.25 × 7 × 1 × 1.3 ≈ 11.4  →  pack 12 candles

Twelve candles out the door; the buffer sits in the back-stock bin until it is table stock. The table is the showroom; the shelf is the warehouse — this distinction is most of this chapter.

Wait, and what's on the shelf?

The bring-formula only works when you know what you own. The shelf count — your SKU list — is a ten-minute Sunday-question for your records stack:

Product lineOn shelfCommitted (table)Truly available
Candles, medium2412 (this event)12
Soap, bars604020
Ornaments880 — make more before next event

If "truly available" is negative for a line you plan to bring, that isn't a math problem; it's a make-or-buy decision with a deadline — write it on the after-event review list the same night, because inventory that happens in the two nights before an event is inventory that happens badly.

The stock split: hero / filler / experiment

What you bring is not what you own. You're curating a subset, and the shape that survives contact with the public is:

TierShare of tableWhat it isWhat it asks of you
Heroabout 70%Your best, most-stocked linePriced by the three-price raft; refilled from back-stock through the day
Fillerabout 25%Companions, seasonal fits, second best-sellersPresent, priced, honest tier 2
Experimentabout 5%The new thing you want to trySmall batch, notable signs, honest line ("just launched")

A table that's 70% hero, then, is one message strong — the ten-foot rule from booth setup is about products too.

Sell-out is a plan, not an accident

The chapter's quiet secret: some stock should sell out. The linework:

  • Deliberate sell-out — a limited holiday edition where you want scarcity ("I only brought 20"), clearly labeled as limited. Customers respect an ending they were warned about; the panic kind of sell-out just teaches them your table is unreliable.
  • Restock-from-the-van — a second box in the car for the line that earned a restock by noon. Bring one van-box per event, and only for hero lines. It's the 1.3 buffer with an escape hatch.
  • The one-third rule — when one third of a line is gone, you restock that line now, from the van or the back bin. A third gone is the point where a table starts looking bare from ten feet away, which is exactly where the booth's whole visual promise is kept or lost.

The count-before-you-pack ritual

The sheet that connects this chapter to the packing checklist, filled in at the packing table (not in the car):

  • ☐ Per line: write bring-formula answer on paper
  • ☐ Count actual units into the totes
  • ☐ Note "on hand after packing" per line (that's your post-event shelf number)
  • ☐ Anything already behind (commits but not made) gets a MAKE-MORE post-it, then goes into the review list

Ten minutes. It kills the "did I pack the candles?" spiral before it starts, and it makes the next event's bring-formula inputs (shelf counts) known data instead of memory sifting through boxes at 11 p.m. the night before you have to leave.

What restock math can't fix

Restock math amplifies good products and can't rescue misfits:

  • If a line never sells at any event, the answer isn't more restock units — it's the tracking chapter's question 5: pricing, placement, or a polite sunset.
  • If event traffic was the problem (not your stock), more units just waste the buffer. The event scorecard is the verdict on the event, before you blame the boxes.
  • If your hero line is heroic because of one fluky seasonal spike only your city's weather creates — seasonality belongs in the crowd factor. Round-honest statement: seasonal rates from two years of the same market beat any formula in this chapter.

And, as ever: if the whole enterprise is producing anxiety rather than margin — boxes you were afraid to open, markets you were afraid to book — the answer is not more formulas. The reseller handbook's quitting politely chapter and this book's after-event review are the two doors out of an overstocked, overrun market year, and both open calmly.
