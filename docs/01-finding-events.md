# Finding the Right Events

Event quality determines everything downstream: your sales, your sanity,
your booth layout, even your paperwork. This chapter teaches you to pick
markets the way you'd pick a restaurant — look at who's already in line,
skip the places with no line and a big sign.

## The four kinds of events (and what they cost, roughly)

| Event type | Typical vibe | Booth cost, rounded | First-timer? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Farmers market | Weekly, food-heavy, regulars | Free to about $25/week | Great start |
| Vendor/craft fair | One-day, community-run | About $25-$75 | Great start |
| Holiday market | Seasonal, gift-buyers | About $50-$200 | Good second or third event |
| Convention/expo | Two+ days, themed, far away | About $150-$600 | Graduate level |

Round numbers only — real fees vary hugely by city, table size, and
whether electricity is included. The point of the table is the *shape*:
weekly markets are cheap and forgiving; conventions are expensive and
demand real restock (see [Restock math](07-restock-math.md) for how much
more stock a two-day show eats).

## The five-question event test

Before you pay any fee, you should be able to answer these five
questions. If three or more are "no idea," you're not ready to apply —
you're ready to scout.

1. **Who shops here?** Not "lots of people" — *which* people. Gift-buyers?
   handmade hunters? Parents? Collectors? Your products need to match the
   crowd, not just the fair.
2. **What does it cost all-in?** Booth fee + table rental + electricity
   + parking + website-to-print permit costs + lunch. A $75 booth with
   $40 of extras is a $115 event.
3. **What did last year look like?** Organizers who can't or won't tell
   you attendance counts, layout plans, or vendor return rates are
   telling you something.
4. **Does the event promise fit my workload?** A 10-hour Saturday market
   with 4 a.m. load-in is a different job than a 3-hour evening show.
5. **What do I want from it?** Some events are for money. Some are for
   contacts, photos, portfolio, tests of a new product line. Name your
   goal *before* the event, or you'll review it badly later.

## Scouting: the free visit

Your cheapest research tool is your own feet, applied on a Saturday.
Visit a market *as a shopper first* and walk it with the [event
scorecard](event-scorecards.md) in your pocket:

- **Count the tables.** Ten vendors is a quiet show; two hundred is a
  destination. Count aisles, count people per aisle, count people
  *carrying bags* (carriers buy).
- **Watch for money hands.** Are buyers walking out with items? Watch
  three sellers for five minutes each: are they talking to browsers or
  to each other?
- **Notice what sells.** Crowds cluster around booths with: visible
  prices, hands-on demonstrations, and clearly labeled one-thing-per-row
  tables. Crowds *skim* booths with no prices ("hmm, do I have to
  ask?").
- **Talk to three vendors on their break.** Most will tell you: how's
  traffic, what's the fee, would you return? Ask the *biggest* booth
  and the *smallest* — they experience this market differently.
- **Take photos of anything that catches you.** Colors, layouts, ideas
  you'd steal honestly later.

The scorecard page has a printable version of all this — a **pre-event
scorecard** you can fill in at the fair with a pencil.

## Questions to ask the organizer (before you pay)

Organizers expect these questions, and good organizers answer them
cheerfully. Ask for:

1. **Is electricity available, and does it cost extra?**
2. **Is the show indoors, outdoors, or covered — and what's the weather
   rule if it rains?** (Do vendors get refunds, free next-year fees,
   or nothing?)
3. **What's the load-in time and parking situation?**
4. **Are tables and chairs provided or rented?**
5. **Are you jurying applications?** (Some events hand-pick their mix of
   vendors to avoid ten candle tables.)
6. **Is there a waitlist, and have returning vendors already taken the
   good spots?**
7. **What paperwork do you require from me?** (See the
   [permits chapter](02-applications-and-permits.md) — this question is
   a feature, not a bug, and it makes you sound professional. If they
   answer with words like "jury," "load-in," or "COI," the
   [Glossary](glossary.md) decodes them.)

## Event red flags

Some "events" are scams wearing an event costume. Walk away from:

- **Upfront "vendor kits" or mandatory purchases.** Pay-to-be-in-Lineup
  plus "here's the exact signage you must buy from *us*" is a real scam
  pattern.
- **No public evidence of past years.** No photos from last year, no
  press, no reviews from previous vendors. An event that's brand new is
  fine to *watch*, not fine to *fund*.
- **Payment requests by cash app or prepaid card** to "reserve your
  spot." Legitimate events take checks, direct forms, or standard
  payment methods, and they can't be resold to you mid-conversation.
- **Overpayment tricks.** Anyone sending you a check for too much and
  asking you to refund the difference is a scammer. Cut it. It's the
  same fake-check bounce mechanism the reseller book warns about:
  their check shows bogus funds in your account, then bounces, after
  you already handed over the "refund."
- **Pressure.** "Last spot! Pay now!" messages at 9 p.m. Real event
  deadlines are calm and posted.

## Start small on purpose

Your first event should be:

- **One day.** Two-day shows take two-day restocks, two sleep cycles,
  and two-day weather risk.
- **Cheap.** A $25 table at a farmers market teaches you what a $400
  art-fair booth would: pricing, change-making, display strength, your
  feet wearing out. No shame — that's how everyone starts.
- **Close.** Within an hour's drive. You want to be able to go home and
  grab that thing you forgot, and you will forget something (see the
  [packing checklist](packing-checklist.md), and check it the night
  before).
- **Small stakes.** A first event that breaks even after fees is a
  *successful* first event if it also taught you what your booth
  actually needs.

## The event calendar rhythm

Most regions have a predictable season:

- **Spring**: garden shows, spring festivals, Mother's Day markets.
- **Summer**: weekly farmers markets, street fairs, county fairs.
- **Fall**: harvest shows, back-to-school vendor nights.
- **Holiday season**: the big one — November and December markets are
  where gift-buyers congregate and budgets are warm.

Recurring markets (weekly or monthly) are *lifeboats*: same organizer,
same table, regular customers who get to know you. One-offs are
*excursions*: higher highs, lower lows. A healthy year mixes both —
maybe one recurring market that reliably pays for itself, plus four to
six one-day excursions that test new products and neighborhoods.

Your fifth event doesn't have to be your fifth *kind* of event. When a
market works, book it again next season with the same setup and a life
less spent on load-in panic.
