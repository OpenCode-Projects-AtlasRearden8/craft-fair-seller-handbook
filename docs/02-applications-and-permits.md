# Applications and Permits (Plain English)

This is the chapter people skip and then panic about on Thursday night.
Here's the friendly version: what's on an application, what an organizer
is actually asking for when they mention permits, and what a sales-tax
permit is in words you can say out loud. One clear flag on everything:
this book is education, not tax or legal advice — when a question has
real money or real law in it, a real professional or the actual agency
gives the real answer.

## What's on an application form (and why)

Event applications are mostly *matchmaking* questions. Organizers are
trying to build a good room. Expect:

| Question | What it's really asking |
| --- | --- |
| Business / seller name | "Whose name goes on the vendor list?" (See *Business names* below.) |
| Describe your work | "Will you fit the vibe? Will you be different from the eight other candle tables?" |
| 3-5 photos of your work or booth | "Is this real, does it photograph well?" (Booth photos beat product photos — organizers imagine *your setup* in *their room*.) |
| Table/chair/electricity needs | "How much room and power do we budget for you?" |
| Booth space size requested | "Single, double, corner, with-wall?" |
| Insurance question | "If you knock over a customer with a canopy leg, is there a policy, or is it *our* problem?" |
| W-9 request | "Our accountant needs a vendor record." (Plain-English note below.) |
| Payment of booth fee | The actual transaction. |

Applications for juried shows add: photos of you or your process ("is
this handmade?"), short artist statements ("what makes it yours?"), and
deadlines measured in months, not days.

## The two rules of applying

1. **Apply early.** Returning vendors often get re-booking priority
   before the public opens. The best spots go to whoever applied in
   January for the October event, not whoever applied in September.
2. **Waitlist is not rejection.** A waitlist means you're in the queue
   with a chance to move up, which happens constantly — babysitters
   back out, mortgages cash in, a mechanical bull cancels. Say yes to
   waitlist offers within a day or two of being asked; they're often
   last-minute.

Read the *vendor contract* before you pay the fee. Look for the
cancellation policy (both directions), the refund rule for rain, the
load-in/load-out times, any marketing requirements (logo use,
social-media posts), and the terms for being bumped or re-seated.
Sign only what you understand — that sentence is the most practical
legal advice in this book, and it isn't legal advice.

## Business names, LLCs, and the way they show up on forms

"LLC-flavored" means this book assumes a small formal business *might*
exist. On an application:

- If you have a formal **business name** — say, an LLC name — you
  apply and appear in the vendor list under that registered name.
  That's generally what organizers want: the name that matches
  whatever permits, insurance, or bank account you show.
- If your business is informal (you sell under your own personal
  name), that's fine too, and the application just gets your real
  name. Nothing here tells you whether to form an LLC — that's a
  sit-down conversation with a real professional, and it depends on
  your situation, income, risk, and state. What this book *can* tell
  you: whichever name you use, use it **everywhere** — application,
  permit records, tax form you give, and the name on your
  card-reader profile. Mismatched names cause more Friday-afternoon
  confusion than any other paperwork detail.
- **DBA / trade name**: if you sell under a display name ("Dylan's
  Unruly Wooden Things") that's different from your legal business
  name, some events ask about both. Write the legal name as the business
  name and the display name in the "describe your work" or "brand name"
  slot if one exists. If no display-name field exists, the "describe
  your work" text is the right place to slip it in.

## The W-9, in one paragraph

A **W-9** is not a tax payment and not an IRS audit. It's a simple form
you fill out so a paying business — here, the event organizer — can,
in *some* situations, issue *certain* paperwork to its accountant later.
It asks your name (and business name, if different), address, and your
**TIN** — which, for most small vendors, is either your Social Security
number (as an individual) or an **EIN**, the nine-digit Employer
Identification Number you can get free from the IRS when you have a
business identity you'd rather use than your personal number. Two
practical notes, no advice attached: first, giving the *right name
with the TIN* matters (again: use the same name everywhere); second,
W-9s with SSNs are sensitive personal documents — hand them to a
verified organizer through the organizer's own channel, never to "the
person who messaged you about booth setup."

## Insurance — what the question means, and who to ask

When an application asks **"Do you carry vendor liability insurance?"**,
it's asking whether you (not the venue, not the organizer) have a
policy that would respond if someone were hurt by your booth or your
product. Organizers often *require* proof of it for the bigger shows,
and the industry informally calls it a **COI** ("certificate of
insurance").

Plain-English framing only:

- Small community shows frequently *don't* require it; large juried
  fairs and conventions often do. The application tells you which kind
  you're in.
- Organizers are allowed to require it, and they're not the people who
  sell it — they simply tell you their requirement; the *sourcing*
  decision is between you and a licensed professional (and, if you
  operate one, your LLC's paperwork is where the policy name goes).
- Policy costs and coverage vary wildly by state, product, and the
  venue's rules. Do **not** let a chapter of a handbook choose your
  insurance — let this chapter tell you *which question to ask*, and
  let a licensed agent give the real quote. The wrong insurance isn't
  just bad value; no insurance can also mean a Friday cancellation you
  saw too late.

## Sales-tax permits, in genuinely plain English

Here's what's really being asked when a form says "Provide seller's
permit number."

The idea in one breath: most U.S. states levy a **sales tax** on things
consumers buy, and the rule is roughly — *you* collect it from
customers and then hand it (remit it) to the state, usually monthly or
quarterly. For a fair or market, the practical version is:

1. **Most states want sellers of taxable goods to be registered with
   the state's tax department** before they sell. The specific form and
   name vary hugely by state ("sales tax permit," "seller's permit,"
   "resale certificate," "certificate of registration" — synonyms
   without a standard meaning; the state's own **department of
   revenue** website is always the real source of truth, full stop).
2. **Registration is usually simple and often free.** It's a form, and
   (educationally — not reliably, so check your state's current
   reality) many states process small registrations in a few business
   days. Some states only ask you to register for a *temporary*
   window: "a permit for this one event." Other states use one
   year-round registration for everything. Some states have no general
   sales tax at all (a handful do not, at the general level). This book
   deliberately does not name your state; the state does, definitively,
   on its own site.
3. **The organizer uses that registration number to satisfy *their*
   records** — they often need to show their venue or town office that
   their vendors are registered, not running anonymous tables. It's a
   checkbox, not an exam. Number in hand goes on the form, done.
4. **At the booth:** when a customer buys a taxable item, you add the
   local rate to the sale (a poster of "$1 tax per $10" sits behind
   you — see [Pricing](04-pricing-and-making-change.md)). The money
   collected belongs to the state as soon as it lands in the cash box,
   on paper — *most vendors don't physically split it out, they just
   record it*, then remit what they collected at filing time.
5. **At the end of the period**, you file the state's return (many are
   now quick online forms you can finish during a lunch break) and
   send in what was collected. When in doubt about any of it, the
   state's own help line answers seller-permit and filing questions
   plainly, and genuinely more kindly than you probably expect.

**The one-phone-call rule:** *before* you pay a booth fee for your very
first event, make one phone call to your state's department of revenue
(the number is on their official site, not in this book) and ask two
plain questions:

1. "I'm selling at a one-day craft fair in your town — do I need to
   register, and is it free?"
2. "Is the permit permanent, or do I need a temporary one per event?"

That phone call settles more than ten blog posts, including this one.
Write the answers on the inside cover of your [money kit](packing-checklist.md)
and you're equipped. If the phone line is long, most states also have
the answers on their official site, in plain HTML, no login required.

## The "what do I bring" checklist (paperwork edition)

Structure says "bring copies even if nothing checks them," and that's
good advice. Into the [paperwork kit](packing-checklist.md):

- [ ] Your [sales-tax permit](#sales-tax-permits-in-genuinely-plain-english)
      registration or number, on paper
- [ ] The COI if the event required one
- [ ] A filled, signed copy of the booth-fee receipt (proof you paid)
- [ ] A copy of the vendor contract you signed
- [ ] One W-9 form, *blank*, in case you meet someone who needs one
      signed in person
- [ ] A photo ID
- [ ] Your **event pass / load-in pass** if the event issues them

None of this takes space. A plastic sleeve holds it all.

## If the application mentions "fees for taxes"

Some events charge *additional* fees on top of booth rent with labels
like "taxes," "license fee," or "towns tax collected by organizer."
These exist legitimately in some jurisdictions where the venue collects
and remits city-level taxes on behalf of vendors instead of making each
vendor do the individual paperwork — genuinely common around
tourist-heavy markets. Whether **that model applies in your town**, and
whether **you're still responsible for something else**, is exactly the
kind of thing the **one-phone-call rule** (just above) was
made for. Ask the organizer first (they usually explain it with
enthusiasm — it's their paperwork saving you hours), and only call the
state when the organizer's answer is unclear.

## The short version, for a sticky note
1. **Apply early; waitlist is not rejection.**
2. **Same name everywhere** — application, permit, card reader.
3. **W-9 is a form, not a payment.** SSN or [EIN](glossary.md);
   an EIN is free from the IRS and keeps your personal number out
   of organizers' files.
4. **Insurance question = "do you have a COI?"** Only an agent quotes.
5. **Sales-tax permit**: register with your state before you sell;
   call the state's department of revenue once with two questions;
   write the answers inside your money kit.
6. **Bring paper copies** of everything to the event.
