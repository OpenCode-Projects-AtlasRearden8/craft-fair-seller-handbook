# Slow Days and Weather Survival

Two things happen on market days that no spreadsheet predicts: the
crowd doesn't come, and the sky goes sideways. This chapter is for
both — ten moves for the slow hours, a real rain plan, and the one
rule that outranks both: the seller is also at the event, and the
seller needs lunch.

## Slow hours are normal hours

Most booths — most good booths, veteran ones — sit through several
ghost hours a day. Traffic comes in waves (church lets out, lunch
happens, kids get bored and drag buyers past your table). A dead
hour is *not a verdict on the booth*, which is worth knowing
because panic-calming is one of the things that makes booths look
desperate. The moves below work best when done in the order your
mood needs, not all in one sitting.

## Ten slow-hour moves (in the order that usually works)

1. **Straighten the table like a neighbor is coming.** Hands busy,
   brain kind. Tap in a stray item, re-fold a cloth corner. Motion
   without hovering.
2. **Walk your own booth as a visitor.** Step ten feet out. Squint
   prices. What's missing? What's buried? Fix one thing.
3. **Craft or restock *visible hands*.** A visible hand at work
   (folding, carving, packing, sorting) is a demonstration and a
   conversation opener. This is the oldest market trick: watching
   someone make the thing *is the ad*.
4. **Greet the browsers with a sentence, not a pitch.** "Feel free
   to pick them up — they're hand-poured." One line, then zero
   hovering. The hover is the fast way to lose someone who was
   interested.
5. **Chat with the booth across the aisle** when it's calm. Trade
   business cards, mention you liked their display, swap the local
   knowledge (which events they did, what died this year). Slow
   hours build most of a vendor's future event intel.
6. **Give the table a small refresh.** Move the hero display two
   inches taller, rotate the front row, retape a price card.
   Movement catches ten-second glances; solidity reads as closed.
7. **Ask the organizer for data.** "How's the day looking?" gets
   you honest information (some shows have *waves* — a stage act at
   2 p.m. means you staff up then, not now).
8. **Offer one planned gesture, only if you planned it.** "Last
   hour, gift wrap free" — something you decided at
   [review time](09-after-event-review.md) last event, not a
   2:30 p.m. panic discount. Panic discounts teach buyers to shop
   at 2:30 p.m.
9. **Tend yourself.** Water, snack, stretch, sunscreen after
   sunrise hours, layers when it cools. An un-tended seller starts
   making sloppy money moves (see [pricing](04-pricing-and-making-change.md)).
10. **Write one line in the tally sheet.** "Dead till 1:30, then
    wave." By the [after-event review](09-after-event-review.md)
    that line becomes a booking argument for the calendar; in the
    moment it's just honest handwriting.

And the mental note that resets the day: **you already accounted
for slow hours in restock math and the event scorecard.** The day
was never going to be nine hours of sales; the [bring-formula](07-restock-math.md)
just doesn't care about your noon feelings.

## Weather: the canopy becomes a tent

The one upgrade that turns weather from an event-killer into a
hassle: treat the canopy as a **weather machine** with a floor, a
wall, and a roof that works:

- **Weights at all four legs — before setup finishes.** This is
  the only life-safety note in the whole book: a weighted canopy
  in real wind is a tent; an unweighted one is a hazard. (Kits and
  free water-jug versions in [booth setup](03-booth-setup-on-a-budget.md).)
- **Sidewalls on the weather side**, one or two is plenty. Zip
  ties, [bungees](glossary.md). On rain days, the windward
  [sidewall](glossary.md) keeps both the product and the customers
  dry-ish.
- **A gutter move for sudden rain:** two canopies slightly offset
  share the seam; *one* canopy alone: lower the back two legs a
  couple of inches so water runs off backward instead of pooling.
  (Pool-over-collapse is the real rain risk; keep the roof angled.)
- **Never raise or drop a canopy mid-gust.** Take product off
  *first*, drop the **two side legs first** with someone holding
  the frame, and only then touch the middle. Wind is the same
  fight at 10 a.m. and 3 p.m.: patience beats strength.
- **Indoor event rain risk is mostly comfort:** if the fair is
  inside, rain mostly means *fewer* shoppers; the carry-rain-gear
  items below keep your stock dry *between* car and hall.

## The wet-weather kit (put it in Tote 1)

- Rain covers or a tarp for the product table (a shower-curtain
  liner works and costs $5)
- One towel for the cloth-to-metal (canopy poles weep in real
  rain — yes, really)
- Ziplock bags for anything paper (price tags, cash, business
  cards, the [tally sheet](06-honest-sales-tracking.md))
- The good shoes. Dry socks. The seller's feet are the
  forgotten PPE of markets.
- A **wind plan for the light stuff**: paper tags fly at gust #3;
  nudging them into clothespins-on-string stops that before it
  begins.

## The go/no-go call (before you leave the house)

The [vendor contract](02-applications-and-permits.md) you signed in
chapter 2 answers most of this: read it again, because the refund-rain
rule is usually in there. Then:

1. **What does the event do?** (Cancel, delay, shelter-in-place,
   "the show goes on.") Most organizers decide by loading time, and
   honest ones reply by 6 a.m.
2. **What's the actual forecast risk?** If your buyer crowd won't
   come ("the act canceled, the trail race called it"), even a
   dry canopy is a slow day — see the [slow-hour moves](#ten-slow-hour-moves-in-the-order-that-usually-works).
3. **Is your booth weather-rated?** A $60 pop-up with no walls is
   not the same machine as a $400 show shelter with sidewalls.
   Grade your canopy honestly before deciding.
4. **Go/no-go is a phone call, not a debate.** "Is the show still
   on? My kit and I are deciding at 5:30." Do not drive
   weather-tests at 5 a.m. with a loaded van and no answer; call,
   wait, then decide.

A no-go that *saved* a day's drive and the stock from a drowned
booth is a money-profile event, and the [after-event
review](09-after-event-review.md) loves one.

## Rain-day economics, in round numbers

If it looks like a washout and the event goes on anyway, your
options with a booth: **show up with the covered kit and eat the
slow day (which is not free — goods you didn't sell at a washout
can still be listed online, resold-book-style, and the day's fee is
sunk anyway)**, or **stay home and eat the booth fee**. The math:

- Show up: booth fee spent + a small chance of real rain-period
  sales, with risk of rain-damage to stock you didn't cover.
- Stay home: fee spent, stock dry, the day gets used for making,
  listing, and *rest* — which a season of back-to-back events
  might honestly need.

Round honest answer: **a prepared booth wins more often than a
bailed day**, but if the forecast is a genuine storm (not rain),
the tent loses, and the fee is tuition you don't pay twice. A
[wet go/no-go rule](event-scorecards.md) that you write *before*
the season is how you make each wet call with less stress.

The no-secret coda: please actually eat breakfast. The rain-day
buyer who *does* appear deserves a warm hello, and needs the
seller fed and rested too. That's all of it — the show goes on, and
you go on with it.
